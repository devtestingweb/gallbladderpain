Gallbladder Attack: Symptoms, How Long It Lasts and What to Do

By The GallbladderPain.org editorial teamUpdated 3 November 2026

A gallbladder attack can be one of the most painful things you've ever felt, and it often strikes without warning in the middle of the night. Here's how to recognise one, what to do while it's happening, and how to make the next one less likely.

When to get help straight away

Get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes. Call 999 or go to A&E if you have:

  • sudden, severe tummy pain
  • pain spreading to your back, with vomiting
  • a very high temperature, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
  • yellow skin or yellowing in the whites of your eyes

Don't drive yourself. No food or drink on this site replaces medical care.

What a gallbladder attack feels like

Doctors call a typical attack biliary colic. It usually follows the same pattern:

  • Sudden, severe pain under your ribs on the right, or in the middle of your upper tummy
  • It builds and stays steady, rather than coming in waves
  • It can spread to your back or right shoulder blade
  • Nothing eases it: changing position, passing wind, going to the toilet or being sick doesn't help
  • Sweating, feeling sick or vomiting are common

Most attacks ease off within a few hours, once the stone moves or the gallbladder relaxes. If the pain lasts longer than 30 minutes, the NHS advises getting urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111. Pain that goes on for many hours, especially with a fever, can mean your gallbladder has become inflamed.

Why attacks happen

Your gallbladder stores bile and squeezes it out when you eat, especially when you eat fat. If a gallstone is sitting at the exit, that squeeze pushes against a blocked opening, and that's the pain. It's why attacks so often follow a big or fatty meal, and why they're common in the evening and at night after the biggest meal of the day.

What to do during a gallbladder attack

  1. Check the warning signs. Call 999 if the pain is sudden and severe, spreads to your back with vomiting, or comes with a high temperature, shivering or yellow skin or eyes.
  2. Get advice if it lasts. If the pain lasts longer than 30 minutes, get an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111.
  3. Stop eating. Don't eat while the pain is severe. Small sips of water are fine.
  4. Ask about pain relief. A pharmacist, GP or NHS 111 can tell you which painkillers are safe for you.
  5. Make a note. Write down when it started, how long it lasted and what you last ate. It helps your doctor, and it helps you spot your own triggers.

After the attack: the next 48 hours

Your gallbladder area may feel sore and bruised for a day or two. Give it an easy time with small, plain, low-fat meals: porridge, dry toast, rice, plain potatoes, broth-based soups, steamed vegetables, skinless chicken and white fish. Bring other foods back gradually. Book a GP appointment to talk about what happened, even if you feel fine now.

Making the next attack less likely

  • Eat smaller meals, more regularly, and don't skip breakfast
  • Make your evening meal a lighter one
  • Cut back on the big triggers: fried food, takeaways, fatty and processed meat, cream, butter and full-fat cheese
  • Spread any fat you eat across the day instead of having it in one meal

If attacks keep coming back, the usual treatment is keyhole surgery to remove the gallbladder. Your GP can refer you. In the meantime, food is the thing most in your control, and some foods are genuinely kind to a sore gallbladder.

Questions people ask

How long does a gallbladder attack last?

Usually from around 30 minutes to a few hours, then it gradually eases. The NHS advises getting urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes.

What does a gallbladder attack feel like?

A sudden, severe, steady pain under the right ribs or in the middle of the upper tummy, which can spread to your back or right shoulder blade. Many people feel sick, vomit or sweat. Moving, passing wind or going to the toilet doesn't usually ease it.

What triggers a gallbladder attack?

Attacks often come 30 minutes to an hour after eating, especially after a large or fatty meal, and often at night. Fried food, takeaways, fatty meat and full-fat dairy are the triggers people mention most.

What should I eat after a gallbladder attack?

Keep to small, plain, low-fat meals for a day or two: porridge made with water or skimmed milk, toast, rice, plain potatoes, broth-based soup, bananas and steamed vegetables. Bring other foods back slowly.

Is a gallbladder flare-up the same as an attack?

People use "flare-up" in different ways, but usually they mean the same thing as an attack: a bout of gallbladder pain. If you have pain on and off for days, or pain that doesn't settle, see your GP.

Will I need surgery after a gallbladder attack?

Not necessarily after one attack. But if attacks keep coming back, the usual treatment is keyhole surgery to remove the gallbladder. Your GP will talk through the options.

One food to choose

Bananas

When you're recovering from an attack and don't know what's safe, reach for a ripe banana. It has almost no fat (about 0.4g), it's soft and easy to digest, and it's on every gentle-foods list for flare days. Slice one onto porridge made with water or skimmed milk for one of the kindest breakfasts you can have.
