Foods to Avoid with Gallstones: The Complete List

By The GallbladderPain.org editorial teamUpdated 3 November 2026

If you've just been told you have gallstones, this is the list to start with. These are the foods most likely to bring on pain, and for every one of them there's an easy swap that lets you keep enjoying your meals.

Why these foods cause gallstone pain

Your gallbladder is a small pouch under your liver that stores bile, a fluid that helps you digest fat. When fat arrives in your gut, your body releases a hormone called cholecystokinin (CCK), which tells the gallbladder to squeeze bile out.

If a gallstone is sitting near the exit, that squeeze can press the stone against the opening or push it into the duct. That's what causes the sudden, severe pain in the upper right or middle of your tummy, known as biliary colic. The more fat in a meal, the stronger the squeeze. That's why a fry-up or a takeaway is far more likely to cause an attack than a bowl of porridge.

1. Fried food

Frying soaks food in oil. A plain boiled potato has almost no fat; the same potato as chip-shop chips can carry a meal's worth. Battered fish, fried chicken, onion rings, spring rolls, samosas and doughnuts all fall into this group.

Swap it: oven-baked or air-fried chips with a teaspoon of oil, grilled fish, breadcrumbed chicken baked in the oven. Read the full guide to fried food and gallstones.

2. Whole milk and full-fat dairy

Dairy fat is one of the most common hidden triggers because it turns up in so many everyday things: milk in tea and coffee, cheese in sandwiches, butter on toast, cream in sauces. Whole milk, cream, butter, ice cream and most hard and soft cheeses are all high in fat.

Swap it: skimmed or 1% milk, fat-free Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese or quark, and a thin scrape of spread instead of butter. See our guides to milk and cheese.

3. Fatty and processed meat

Bacon, sausages, burgers, salami, pepperoni, chorizo, pork pies, ribs, lamb chops and fatty mince are all high in saturated fat. Processed meats are often fried as well, which doubles up the problem.

Swap it: skinless chicken or turkey, lean ham, 5%-fat mince, white fish, beans and lentils. Trim any visible fat before cooking.

4. Takeaways and fast food

A burger and fries, a large pizza, a kebab or a creamy curry with pilau rice and naan can each carry more fat than you'd normally eat in a whole day. Takeaways are one of the most common triggers people report, often late in the evening.

Swap it: a tomato-based curry with boiled rice, a thin-crust pizza with vegetable toppings and half the cheese, a grilled chicken wrap, or sushi.

5. Pastries, cakes and biscuits

Croissants, pain au chocolat, sausage rolls, pies, cream cakes, muffins and many biscuits are made with lots of butter or other fat. Because they're sweet or snack-sized, it's easy to forget how rich they are.

Swap it: a toasted teacake or crumpet with jam, malt loaf, a meringue with berries, or fruit with fat-free yoghurt.

6. Creamy sauces, gravies and soups

Carbonara, korma, cheese sauce, hollandaise, creamy mushroom soup and meat gravy made from the roasting fat can turn a light meal into a heavy one.

Swap it: tomato-based pasta sauces, stock-based gravy, broth-based soups and a squeeze of lemon or herbs for flavour.

7. Big, heavy meals

Portion size matters as much as the food itself. A huge Sunday roast or an all-you-can-eat buffet asks your gallbladder to work hard all at once, even if each item is fairly healthy.

Swap it: eat smaller meals more often. Three modest meals and one or two light snacks suit most people well.

What you can still eat

Plenty. Fruit, vegetables, oats, rice, pasta, potatoes (not fried), bread, beans and lentils, skinless chicken and turkey, white fish, egg whites, and skimmed or 1% milk are all gallbladder-friendly. The bottom line

You don't need to give up fat completely. Avoid the big hitters (fried food, fatty meat, takeaways, cream, butter and full-fat cheese), keep portions modest, and spread what fat you do eat across the day. Most people find their attacks become much less frequent once they do.

When to get help straight away

Get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes. Call 999 or go to A&E if you have:

  • sudden, severe tummy pain
  • pain spreading to your back, with vomiting
  • a very high temperature, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
  • yellow skin or yellowing in the whites of your eyes

Don't drive yourself. No food or drink on this site replaces medical care.

Questions people ask

What foods trigger gallbladder attacks?

The most common triggers are large meals that are high in fat: fried food, takeaways, fatty and processed meat, full-fat dairy such as cheese, cream and butter, pastries and creamy sauces. Everyone's triggers are a little different, so a food diary helps you find yours.

Do I have to avoid fat completely with gallstones?

No. NHS dietitians say a strict low-fat diet isn't needed for everyone. Small amounts of healthy fats, such as a teaspoon of olive oil or a small handful of nuts, are part of a balanced diet. It's large amounts of fat in one meal that cause most problems.

Is milk bad for gallstones?

Whole milk is a higher-fat food, so it's best limited. Skimmed and 1% milk are low in fat and are fine for most people. See our full guide to milk and gallstones.

Can I eat cheese with gallstones?

Most hard cheeses are around a third fat, so keep portions small. Cottage cheese, quark and reduced-fat cheeses are much lighter options. See our full guide to cheese and gallstones.

Are eggs on the list of foods to avoid?

Not for most people. Egg whites are almost fat-free. Whole eggs are fine one at a time, boiled or poached. Fried eggs are the version to limit.

Should I avoid spicy food with gallstones?

Spicy food isn't a direct gallstone trigger, but it can upset a sensitive stomach and is often served in rich, oily dishes like curries. Choose tomato-based dishes over creamy ones and see how you get on.

Is chocolate bad for gallstones?

Most chocolate is around 30% fat, so a whole bar counts as a fatty snack. A couple of squares of dark chocolate is usually fine. Chocolate cake, cream-filled bars and chocolate mousse are better kept as rare treats.
