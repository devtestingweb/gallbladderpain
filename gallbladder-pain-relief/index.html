Gallbladder Pain Relief: What Actually Helps (and What Doesn't)

By The GallbladderPain.org editorial teamUpdated 3 November 2026

When gallbladder pain hits, all you want is for it to stop. This guide tells you what really helps, what's a waste of time, and when pain relief isn't enough and you need a doctor.

When to get help straight away

Get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes. Call 999 or go to A&E if you have:

  • sudden, severe tummy pain
  • pain spreading to your back, with vomiting
  • a very high temperature, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
  • yellow skin or yellowing in the whites of your eyes

Don't drive yourself. No food or drink on this site replaces medical care.

First: is this an emergency?

Before you think about painkillers, check the warning signs above. Painkillers can hide how bad things are, so it's important to know when to stop trying to manage at home.

If you have...Do this
Sudden, severe tummy painCall 999 or go to A&E
Pain spreading to your back, and you're being sickCall 999 or go to A&E
A high temperature, or you feel hot, cold and shiveryCall 999 or go to A&E
Yellow skin, or yellow in the whites of your eyesCall 999 or go to A&E
Chest pain, or pain spreading to your arm, neck or jawCall 999
Tummy pain lasting longer than 30 minutesGet an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111

Don't drive yourself to hospital. Ask someone to take you, or call an ambulance.

What to do during a gallbladder attack

If none of the emergency signs apply, here's what helps most people get through an attack.

1. Stop eating

Food, especially fatty food, makes your gallbladder squeeze. While you're in pain, give it a rest. Don't eat anything until the pain has gone.

2. Sip water

Small sips of water are fine and help if you've been sick. Avoid alcohol, fizzy drinks and anything creamy or milky.

3. Take a painkiller that's safe for you

We cover which ones in the next section.

4. Get as comfortable as you can

There's no special position that's proven to help gallbladder pain. Most people just find the position that hurts least, whether that's lying on their side, sitting up or curling up. Some people find gentle warmth from a hot water bottle (wrapped in a towel, never too hot) comforting. It won't fix anything, but it may help you feel a little better.

5. Keep track of time

Note when the pain started. If it lasts longer than 30 minutes, the NHS says to get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111.

6. Write it down afterwards

Once it's over, jot down what you ate, when the pain started, where it hurt and how long it lasted. This helps your GP, and helps you spot your own trigger foods.

Which painkillers help with gallbladder pain?

The NHS mentions two everyday painkillers for gallstone pain while you're waiting for treatment:

  • Paracetamol
  • Ibuprofen

If the pain is severe, your doctor may give you something stronger.

Research suggests that anti-inflammatory painkillers, called NSAIDs (ibuprofen is one of these), work particularly well for gallstone pain, better than medicines that relax the muscles.

Check before you take ibuprofen

Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs aren't safe for everyone. They may not be right for you if you:

  • have had a stomach ulcer or bleeding from your stomach
  • have kidney, liver or heart problems
  • have asthma that's made worse by these medicines
  • take blood thinners or certain other medicines
  • are pregnant

A pharmacist can tell you in a couple of minutes what's safe for you. Always read the leaflet, and never take more than the dose it says.

If your attacks keep coming back, speak to your GP. Painkillers help you cope, but they don't stop the attacks happening.

Home remedies that don't work

If you search online for gallbladder pain relief, you'll find a long list of home remedies. It's natural to want to try something, so here's the honest truth about the most common ones.

RemedyDoes it relieve gallbladder pain?
Apple cider vinegarNo good evidence it helps. It can also upset your stomach.
Lemon waterNo evidence it helps the pain. Water is fine, the lemon doesn't add anything.
Peppermint teaNo evidence for gallbladder pain. Some people find it soothing, which is fine.
Olive oil and juice "flush"No evidence it works, and a big dose of oil can actually trigger an attack.
TurmericNo good evidence for gallstone pain.
Epsom saltsNo evidence for gallstones, and they can cause diarrhoea.

A cup of herbal tea won't hurt you. The danger is when a home remedy makes you wait at home when you should be getting help. If you have any of the warning signs, skip the remedies and get medical advice.

Gallbladder pain at night

Lots of gallbladder attacks happen in the evening or wake people from sleep. That's often because dinner is the biggest meal of the day, and many attacks start 30 minutes to an hour after eating.

If your attacks tend to happen at night, try:

  • making your evening meal smaller and lower in fat
  • having your bigger meal at lunchtime instead
  • leaving a couple of hours between eating and going to bed
  • skipping late-night snacks like crisps, cheese, chocolate or takeaways

After the attack: how to feel better

Once the pain has gone, your tummy might feel sore or bruised for a day or two. Go gently:

  • eat small, plain, low-fat meals, like porridge, toast, rice, plain potatoes or soup
  • bring other foods back slowly, one at a time
  • keep sipping water
  • book a GP appointment to talk about what happened, even if you feel fine now

The best pain relief is preventing the next attack

Painkillers help you get through an attack. But the thing that really makes a difference is having fewer attacks in the first place. For most people, that comes down to food:

  • Eat smaller meals, more often. Big meals make your gallbladder squeeze hard.
  • Don't skip meals. Long gaps let bile build up.
  • Cut back on the worst triggers: fried food, fatty meat, takeaways, cream, butter and full-fat cheese.
  • Spread your fat out across the day instead of having it all at once.

If attacks keep coming back even when you're careful, talk to your GP about treatment options. An operation to remove the gallbladder is the only way to stop attacks for good.

In the meantime, here's one thing many people don't think about when they're in pain.

Questions people ask

What is the best pain relief for gallstones?

The NHS mentions paracetamol and ibuprofen for gallstone pain while you wait for treatment, with stronger painkillers if it's severe. Research suggests anti-inflammatory painkillers like ibuprofen work particularly well, but they're not safe for everyone, so check with a pharmacist first.

How can I relieve gallbladder pain at home?

Stop eating while the pain is bad, sip water, take a painkiller that's safe for you, and rest in whatever position is most comfortable. Some people find a warm (not hot) hot water bottle comforting. If the pain lasts longer than 30 minutes, get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111.

Does a hot water bottle help gallbladder pain?

Some people find gentle warmth comforting, but it won't treat the cause. Don't use anything too hot, and don't let it delay getting help if the pain is severe or doesn't settle.

What position helps gallbladder pain?

There's no position that's proven to help. Most people simply find the position that feels least uncomfortable. If no position helps at all and the pain is severe, that's another reason to get medical help.

Does apple cider vinegar help gallbladder pain?

There's no good evidence that apple cider vinegar, lemon water, peppermint tea or any other home remedy relieves gallbladder pain or treats gallstones.

Should I go to A&E for gallbladder pain?

Call 999 or go to A&E if the pain is sudden and severe, spreads to your back with vomiting, or comes with a high temperature, shivering, or yellow skin or eyes. If the pain lasts longer than 30 minutes, get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111.

Can I drink alcohol with painkillers for gallbladder pain?

Some painkillers, such as those containing codeine, shouldn't be mixed with alcohol. During a flare-up it's best to avoid alcohol completely. Ask your pharmacist about your specific medicine.

One food to avoid

Alcohol during a flare-up

When your gallbladder is playing up, alcohol is best avoided completely. Some painkillers, like those containing codeine, shouldn't be mixed with it. And the food that comes with drinking, like crisps, takeaways and next-morning fry-ups, is exactly what sets off attacks. Once you're feeling better, a small drink with a light meal is fine for many people.
