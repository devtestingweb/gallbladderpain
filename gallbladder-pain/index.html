Gallbladder Pain: Where It Hurts, What Triggers It and When to Get Help

By The GallbladderPain.org editorial teamUpdated 3 November 2026

Gallbladder pain can come on suddenly and frighteningly hard. This guide explains what's happening, how to tell a typical attack from something more serious, and the simple food changes that make the next one less likely.

When to get help straight away

Get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes. Call 999 or go to A&E if you have:

  • sudden, severe tummy pain
  • pain spreading to your back, with vomiting
  • a very high temperature, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
  • yellow skin or yellowing in the whites of your eyes

Don't drive yourself. No food or drink on this site replaces medical care.

What an attack is usually like

After eatingOften 30 to 60 minutes after a meal, especially a fatty one
At nightAttacks commonly wake people up
Weeks apartEpisodes can be weeks or months apart

Doctors call a typical gallstone attack biliary colic. Despite the name, the pain usually isn't the coming-and-going kind. It tends to build up and stay severe and constant, then slowly fade over a few hours. Changing position, passing wind, going to the toilet or being sick doesn't usually ease it. Many people also feel sick, vomit or break out in a sweat.

Where you feel gallbladder pain

Your gallbladder sits under your liver, tucked under the ribs on the right-hand side. That's where most people feel the pain, or in the middle of the upper tummy just below the breastbone. It can spread round to your back and up to your right shoulder blade.

What causes the pain

Your gallbladder stores bile, a fluid that helps you digest fat. When you eat, especially something fatty, your body tells the gallbladder to squeeze and push bile out through a narrow tube. If a gallstone gets in the way and blocks that exit, the squeezing gallbladder pushes against the blockage, and that causes the pain. When the stone shifts or the gallbladder relaxes, the pain fades.

That's why food matters so much. A large, fatty meal makes your gallbladder squeeze harder, and that makes an attack more likely.

When it's more than a normal attack

Sometimes a gallstone causes a more serious problem. These need medical treatment, so get help quickly if you notice them.

ProblemSigns to look out for
Inflamed gallbladder (cholecystitis)Pain that lasts much longer than a normal attack, often many hours, especially with a high temperature
Blocked bile ductYellow skin or yellowing whites of the eyes (jaundice), dark pee, pale stools, itchy skin, fever and shivering
Inflamed pancreas (pancreatitis)Sudden, severe pain in the upper tummy that spreads to the back, often with vomiting

Is it gallbladder pain or something else?

Pain in the upper tummy can have lots of causes, including indigestion, a stomach ulcer, trapped wind, a pulled muscle and, more rarely, the heart. Only a doctor can tell you for certain, usually with an ultrasound scan. A few clues point towards the gallbladder:

  • The pain is under the right ribs or in the middle of the upper tummy, and may spread to your right shoulder blade.
  • It's severe and steady rather than a dull ache.
  • It often follows a fatty meal or wakes you at night.
  • Antacids, passing wind or going to the toilet don't ease it.

Chest pain is different. If you have pain in your chest, pain spreading to your arm, neck or jaw, or you feel short of breath, call 999.

What to do during an attack

  • Check the warning signs at the top of this page. If the pain lasts longer than 30 minutes, get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111.
  • Don't eat while the pain is severe. Small sips of water are fine.
  • Ask a pharmacist, GP or NHS 111 about pain relief that's safe for you, especially if you take other medicines.
  • Note the time it started and what you last ate. It helps your doctor, and helps you spot your triggers.
  • Afterwards, eat gently. Small, low-fat meals such as porridge, toast, rice, soup and bananas for the next day or two.

Making the next attack less likely

You can't dissolve gallstones with food, and nobody should promise you that. But what you eat has a real effect on how often your gallbladder is pushed into an attack:

  • Keep meals small and regular, and don't skip breakfast.
  • Cut back on the big triggers: fried food, fatty meat, takeaways, cream, butter and full-fat cheese.
  • Spread any fat you do eat across the day instead of having it all in one meal.
  • Fill up on fibre from vegetables, fruit, oats, beans and wholegrains.
  • If you need to lose weight, go slowly, no faster than about 1kg (2lb) a week.

Many people are offered surgery to remove the gallbladder if attacks keep coming back. Talk to your GP about whether that's right for you.

Questions people ask

Where is gallbladder pain felt?

Usually at the top of the tummy, just under the ribs on the right-hand side, or in the middle of the upper tummy. It can spread to your back or your right shoulder blade.

How long does gallbladder pain last?

A typical attack lasts from around 30 minutes to a few hours, then gradually eases. The NHS advises getting urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes. Pain that lasts many hours, or comes with a fever, can mean the gallbladder is inflamed and needs treatment.

What does a gallbladder attack feel like?

Most people describe a severe, constant pain that builds up and doesn't ease when you change position, pass wind or go to the toilet. Many people also feel sick, vomit or sweat.

Why does gallbladder pain often happen at night?

Attacks often come 30 minutes to an hour after eating, especially after a fatty meal, and evening meals tend to be the biggest of the day. Many people find their attacks wake them in the night.

Can gallbladder pain go away on its own?

A single attack usually settles by itself within a few hours. But the gallstones are still there, so more attacks are likely. See your GP if you think you've had gallbladder pain, even if it has gone.

Can gallstones make you tired or dizzy?

Many people feel drained and washed out after an attack, and feeling faint or dizzy can happen with severe pain or vomiting. Ongoing tiredness or dizziness has many possible causes, so mention it to your GP rather than assuming it's your gallbladder.

Can gallbladder pain feel like a heart attack?

Pain high in the tummy can sometimes be hard to tell apart from heart pain. If you have chest pain, pain spreading to your arm, neck or jaw, or you feel breathless, call 999 straight away.
