Gallstones Treatment: Every Option Explained in Plain English

By The GallbladderPain.org editorial teamUpdated 3 November 2026

Been told you have gallstones and now you're wondering what happens next? This guide walks you through every treatment option in plain English, so you can understand your choices and ask your doctor the right questions.

When to get help straight away

Get urgent advice from your GP or NHS 111 if tummy pain lasts longer than 30 minutes. Call 999 or go to A&E if you have:

  • sudden, severe tummy pain
  • pain spreading to your back, with vomiting
  • a very high temperature, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
  • yellow skin or yellowing in the whites of your eyes

Don't drive yourself. No food or drink on this site replaces medical care.

Now let's go through each one properly.

Option 1: No treatment at all

This surprises a lot of people. If your gallstones aren't causing any symptoms, you usually don't need any treatment. Lots of people have gallstones for years without knowing. They're often found by accident, for example during a scan for something else.

UK guidelines say that if your stones aren't causing problems, doctors should reassure you rather than treat you. Surgery always has some risks, so it's not worth doing an operation for stones that aren't bothering you.

What you should do is know the signs. If you start getting pain under your right ribs, especially after meals, see your GP. Read about the 10 signs of a gallbladder problem so you know what to look out for.

Option 2: Changing what you eat

Food can't dissolve gallstones. Anyone who tells you otherwise is not being honest with you. But food can make a real difference to how often you get attacks.

Here's why. Fat makes your gallbladder squeeze. If there's a stone in the way, that squeeze hurts. So eating less fat in each meal means your gallbladder doesn't have to squeeze as hard, which can mean fewer and milder attacks.

Some NHS hospitals say that for some people, cutting down on fat reduces their symptoms so much that they don't need any other treatment. For most people, though, diet is something you do alongside treatment, especially while you wait for an operation.

The basics are:

  • eat smaller meals, more regularly, and don't skip breakfast
  • cut back on fried food, fatty meat, takeaways, butter, cream and full-fat cheese
  • fill up on vegetables, fruit, oats, beans and wholegrains
  • if you need to lose weight, do it slowly

Our gallstones diet guide goes into this in lots more detail.

Option 3: Painkillers

Painkillers don't fix gallstones, but they help you get through an attack. The NHS mentions paracetamol and ibuprofen for gallstone pain while you're waiting for treatment, and says stronger painkillers may be given if the pain is severe.

Research suggests anti-inflammatory painkillers like ibuprofen work particularly well for gallstone pain. But they're not safe for everyone. For example, they may not suit you if you have a stomach ulcer, kidney problems, some types of asthma, or you take blood thinners. Always check with a pharmacist or GP first.

We've written a whole guide on gallbladder pain relief if you want to know more.

Option 4: Removing the gallbladder (cholecystectomy)

If your gallstones are causing pain, the main treatment in the UK is an operation to take out your gallbladder. The medical name is a cholecystectomy (say it like "coh-lee-sis-TEK-tuh-mee"). Around 70,000 of these operations are done in England every year, so it's one of the most common operations there is.

Why take out the whole gallbladder, and not just the stones? Because if the gallbladder stays, new stones usually form again. Removing it is the only way to stop the attacks for good.

Keyhole surgery

Most gallbladder operations are done by keyhole surgery (also called laparoscopic surgery). Here's how it works:

  • You're asleep the whole time, under a general anaesthetic.
  • The surgeon makes a few small cuts, usually four: one near your belly button and a few on the right side of your tummy.
  • They gently fill your tummy with gas so they have room to see.
  • A thin tube with a tiny camera goes in through one cut, and small tools go in through the others.
  • The surgeon removes the gallbladder through one of the cuts.

UK guidelines say this should usually be done as a day case, which means many people go home the same day. Some people stay one night.

Open surgery

Sometimes keyhole surgery isn't possible, for example if the gallbladder is very inflamed. Then the surgeon makes one bigger cut in your tummy instead. This is called open surgery. It usually means more pain afterwards and a longer recovery.

Recovery

  • Going home: usually the same day or the next day after keyhole surgery.
  • Back to work: many people go back within 1 to 2 weeks, depending on their job.
  • Heavy lifting and hard exercise: avoid these for about four weeks.
  • Common side effects: feeling sick, indigestion, some tummy pain or diarrhoea. These usually get better on their own.

Get urgent advice after surgery if your pain gets worse, your wound looks red or has pus, your skin or eyes turn yellow, or you get a high temperature.

Can you live without a gallbladder?

Yes, and most people live completely normally. Your liver keeps making bile. Without a gallbladder to store it, the bile just drips straight into your gut instead. Most people can go back to a normal, healthy diet once they've recovered. Some people notice looser poo or more trips to the toilet, especially after fatty meals. This often settles with time.

Option 5: ERCP for stuck stones

Sometimes a gallstone slips out of the gallbladder and gets stuck in the main tube that carries bile to your gut. This tube is called the common bile duct. A stuck stone here can cause yellow skin (jaundice) or an inflamed pancreas.

To get it out, doctors use something called ERCP. You're given medicine to make you sleepy, then a long, thin, bendy tube with a camera on the end is passed through your mouth, down through your stomach, to where the bile duct opens. The doctor widens the opening so the stone can come out.

ERCP is usually done before or after the gallbladder is removed. For people who aren't fit enough for surgery, it can sometimes be the main treatment.

Option 6: Tablets to dissolve gallstones

There is a medicine that can dissolve some gallstones. It's called ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA for short). It's a type of bile acid that slowly breaks down stones made of cholesterol.

It sounds like the perfect answer, but there are big catches:

  • It only works on some stones. They have to be small, made mainly of cholesterol, and still inside the gallbladder.
  • It's slow. You usually have to take it for several months.
  • Stones often come back once you stop taking it.
  • Only a small number of people are offered it, usually those with mild symptoms or very rare attacks, or people who can't have surgery.

If you'd like to avoid surgery, it's fair to ask your doctor whether this could be an option for you. Just know it isn't suitable for most people.

What about shock wave treatment?

You might have heard of using shock waves to break up stones. This is called lithotripsy. It's used a lot for kidney stones, but it's very rarely used for gallstones in the UK today, because keyhole surgery works so much better.

What about natural remedies and "flushes"?

You'll find lots of websites promising to "flush out" or "dissolve" gallstones with olive oil, apple juice, lemon, vinegar or herbs. There's no good evidence that any of these get rid of gallstones.

The popular "gallbladder flush" is a good example. People drink a lot of olive oil and juice, and then find green lumps in the toilet. They think these are stones. In fact, they're usually soft clumps made from the oil itself mixing with digestive juices. Worse, drinking a large amount of oil makes your gallbladder squeeze hard, which can actually set off an attack.

If you try something "natural", please don't let it delay getting proper help, especially if you have any of the warning signs at the top of this page.

Questions to ask your doctor

Taking a list into your appointment helps you remember everything. You could ask:

  1. Are my gallstones definitely causing my symptoms?
  2. Do I need treatment, or can we wait and see?
  3. If I need surgery, will it be keyhole? Will I go home the same day?
  4. How long is the waiting list, and what should I do while I wait?
  5. Could tablets to dissolve the stones work for me?
  6. Which painkillers are safe for me to take during an attack?
  7. What should I eat, and what should I avoid?

What you can do while you wait

Waiting lists for gallbladder surgery can be long. The good news is that while you wait, the NHS and UK guidelines agree on one thing you can do yourself: avoid the foods and drinks that set off your symptoms, and keep your meals low in fat.

Questions people ask

What is the main treatment for gallstones?

If gallstones are causing symptoms, the main treatment in the UK is an operation to remove the gallbladder, usually done by keyhole surgery. If they aren't causing any symptoms, you usually don't need any treatment.

Can gallstones be treated without surgery?

Sometimes. If they cause no symptoms, they can be left alone. Tablets can dissolve some small stones, but only a small number of people are suitable, it takes months, and stones often come back. Changing what you eat can reduce attacks for some people, but it won't get rid of the stones.

Can you live without a gallbladder?

Yes. Your liver still makes bile. It just drips straight into your gut instead of being stored first. Most people go back to a normal diet after recovering, although some notice looser poo for a while.

How long does it take to recover from gallbladder removal?

Most people go home the same day as keyhole surgery, or the next day. Many people go back to work within 1 to 2 weeks, depending on their job. Avoid heavy lifting for a few weeks.

Can gallstones be dissolved with tablets?

A medicine called ursodeoxycholic acid can slowly dissolve some small gallstones made of cholesterol. It has to be taken for months, it only suits a small number of people, and new stones often form after you stop.

What painkillers can I take for gallstones?

The NHS mentions paracetamol and ibuprofen for gallstone pain while you wait for treatment, and stronger painkillers if the pain is severe. Check with a pharmacist which ones are safe for you.

What is ERCP?

ERCP is a test and treatment that uses a thin, bendy tube with a camera, passed through your mouth, to reach the main bile duct. It's used to remove gallstones that have got stuck in that duct.

One food to choose

Egg whites (and eggs, cooked the right way)

Eggs are cheap, filling and full of protein, but nearly all the fat is in the yolk. Egg whites have almost none. An omelette made with one whole egg and two whites gives you a proper meal with the fat of just one egg. When you do have whole eggs, boil or poach them instead of frying.
